Can Someone’s Ill Will Really Harm Us?

I grew up with the idea that another person’s ill will can sometimes affect you.

If a healthy child suddenly became sick, if something beautiful was damaged soon after someone admired it, or if a happy situation unexpectedly went wrong, people sometimes connected the misfortune with another person’s envious attention. In English, this belief is commonly associated with the evil eye.

When I was younger, I did not question it very much. It was simply something I heard around me. But as I think about it now, I find myself asking a different question: Can another person’s ill will really harm us?

Perhaps the answer begins with understanding where ill will comes from.

We all compare ourselves with other people. Someone has something we want—a successful career, a loving relationship, financial security, recognition, or simply a life that seems happier than our own. Seeing that can produce envy.

But envy itself does not necessarily make us bad people. There is a considerable difference between thinking, “I wish I had what she has,” and thinking, “I wish she didn’t have it.”

The first is sometimes called benign envy. Another person’s success makes us aware of something we want for ourselves and can motivate us to work toward it. The second is malicious envy. We begin to resent the fact that someone else has what we want.

The difference looks small on paper, but in human relationships it can be enormous.

Someone else’s happiness can remind us of our own disappointment. Her success reminds me that I feel unsuccessful. Her recognition reminds me that I feel overlooked. Her confidence makes me more conscious of my own insecurity.

She has done nothing to me. Yet instead of asking, “Why does her happiness make me uncomfortable?” it can be easier to turn that discomfort outward.

Why her? What is so special about her? She isn’t as good as people think.

And perhaps eventually: I wouldn’t mind seeing her brought down a little.

This is where envy begins to turn into ill will.

Science does not provide convincing evidence that another person’s envious gaze or negative thoughts can travel through an invisible force and cause illness, accidents, or misfortune. But that does not mean ill will is harmless.

Malicious envy can become gossip. It can become criticism, exclusion, humiliation, withholding support, damaging someone’s reputation, or quietly creating obstacles for them.

Imagine two colleagues. One receives recognition for her work, and the other feels overlooked.

Feeling disappointed is understandable. Even feeling envious is human. But suppose that envy begins to influence the second colleague’s behavior. She points out the other woman’s mistakes while ignoring her achievements. She makes negative comments about her to others, excludes her from important conversations, or withholds useful information.

When an opportunity arises, she does not recommend her, even though she knows she deserves it.

Nothing supernatural has happened. And yet one person’s ill will has begun to affect another person’s life.

The difficulty is that ill will does not always announce itself. We imagine it as someone openly declaring, “I want you to fail.” In reality, it can be much quieter.

It can appear in repeated attempts to undermine someone’s achievements, in satisfaction at their embarrassment, or in gossip disguised as concern.

“I’m only saying this because I’m worried about her…”

Of course, we cannot judge someone’s intentions from a single comment or an awkward reaction. But a repeated pattern of deliberate undermining can damage relationships, reputations, opportunities, and a person’s sense of belonging.

This makes me wonder why another person’s happiness should bother us in the first place.

Someone else’s success does not take success away from me. Someone else being loved does not make me less lovable. Another person receiving recognition does not diminish the value of my own work.

Yet emotionally, life does not always feel that logical.

We often compare ourselves with people whose circumstances resemble our own. A stranger becoming enormously successful might not affect me at all. But someone who studied with me, works beside me, or started at the same level can provoke a stronger reaction.

Their success can begin to feel like a measurement of my own.

Perhaps envy often tells us less about the person we envy and more about something unresolved within ourselves.

And that means it can tell us something useful, too.

If someone’s achievement makes me envious, I can ask myself why. Perhaps it reminds me of an ambition I have neglected. Perhaps someone else’s freedom makes me realize that I want to change something in my own life.

Instead of thinking, “I wish you had less,” I can ask, “What can I do to create more in my own life?”

This brings me back to the belief I grew up with.

I cannot conclude that an envious gaze possesses some invisible destructive power. But I can recognize a very human truth beneath that old fear.

Ill will has consequences—not because thoughts magically enter our lives, but because they can influence how people treat one another.

Envy can remain a passing emotion. It can become motivation. Or it can develop into resentment and actions that genuinely hurt someone else.

Perhaps, then, the most important question is not whether another person’s envy has mysterious power over us.

It is what we do when envy appears in our own hearts.

Do I think, “I wish I had something like that too”?

Or do I think, “I wish you didn’t have it”?

Between those two thoughts lies the difference between wanting more for ourselves and wanting less for someone else.

And perhaps that is where ill will truly begins.
